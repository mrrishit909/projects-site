How accurately can a used car's price be predicted?

A used car's asking price is predicted to a median 15% miss from age, mileage and model (R² 0.79 on cars the fit has not seen, 0.84 if re-listings leak across folds), and an honest 90% range runs 40% below to 66% above the guess: $11,813 wide for the typical car.

Python · pandas · scikit-learn · Ridge regression · Gradient boosting · Grouped cross-validation · Conformal intervals · OpenML 43306 (CC0) · Hand-written recomputeCode on GitHub ↗

Asking prices on Craigslist (no sale prices), one month (4 April to 4 May 2021), US only, clean titles, $500 to $150,000, model year 1995 on. Twins without a usable VIN may remain, so the by-car scores still leak a little. Boosting is untuned; depreciation rates are associations; the interval covers 90% on average, not in every price band. Source: OpenML 43306, CC0 as labelled by its uploader; Craigslist's terms restrict scraping, so only aggregates are published.

01 — The problem

A seller asks a price, and a buyer wants to know if it is fair. How accurately can a used car's price be predicted from its age, mileage, make-model and engine, and how wide is an honest range around the guess?

The task follows GeeksforGeeks' "Car Price Prediction Analysis". The data is 426,880 real Craigslist listings, in which the same car often appears many times, so how the test is cut decides how good the answer looks.

02 — What I built

One month of US listings, scored the honest way:

  • The audit: $0 and $1 prices, placeholder prices, impossible odometers, and the 141,531 re-listings of a car already counted.
  • The curves: depreciation by age and by miles, brand by brand.
  • The models: the mean, OLS, two ridge fits and boosting, scored five ways: random listings, folds by car, one listing per car, unseen make-model, later cars.
  • The questions behind the guess: which features matter (out of fold) and how wide a 90% interval is in dollars.
  • Check: check.py recounts 719 numbers in plain Python, with no data or statistics library.

03 — How it was built

  1. 01

    Download the listings

    OpenML's copy of the Craigslist used-vehicle listings (dataset 43306, CC0 as labelled by its uploader), a 456 MB parquet file fetched with a size check and a checksum. A slim copy without the links and free text is kept locally; nothing from the listings is committed.

    URL = "https://data.openml.org/datasets/0004/43306/dataset_43306.pq"
  2. 02

    Clean the prices and mileages

    Each listing is dropped for the first rule it breaks, in a fixed order, so the counts add up: $0 and tiny prices, prices over $150,000, placeholder prices, missing or pre-1995 years, impossible odometers, missing make or model, and titles that are not clean.

    ("odometer under 100 on a 2019 or older car", (df.odometer < 100) & (df.year <= 2019)),
  3. 03

    Find the same car listed again

    Two listings are one car when they share a usable VIN, or the same make, model, year and a non-round odometer. The links are joined into connected components, and each car keeps one listing. Without this step, a car listed 261 times counts 261 times and its twins sit in both the training and the test fold.

    ncomp, lab = connected_components(g, directed=False)
  4. 04

    Score it five ways

    Folds are an md5 hash of the group (the listing, the car or the make-model), so they can be recomputed by anyone. The mean, an OLS, two ridge fits (normal equations) and gradient boosting are fitted on log price; the out-of-fold predictions give every metric, the permutation importance and the split-conformal intervals.

    return int(hashlib.md5(str(key).encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % K
  5. 05

    Recount it without the libraries

    check.py reads the file with the csv module and recomputes every cleaning count, the re-listing components, the folds, the ridge fits (Cholesky from per-fold sums) and every score, interval and depreciation number; the boosting predictions are stored and every metric built from them is recomputed: 719 numbers to a relative tolerance of 1e-8.

    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1): x[i] = (z[i] - sum(L[k][i] * x[k] for k in range(i + 1, n))) / L[i][i]

04 — Results

Cleaning. 426,880 listings, 330,469 after the rules and 188,938 cars once re-listings are merged. 32,895 listings (7.7% of the file) ask $0 and 9,199 ask $1 to $499, so they are dropped before anything is modelled. 42.8% of the clean listings are a car already counted: the most listed car appears 261 times, 38,241 cars are listed more than once, and the VIN alone would have missed 19,931 of them. The typical car asks $11,720, is 9 years old and has 107,000 miles.

Depreciation. With age and miles fitted together a car loses 7.1% of its asking price a year and 3.5% per 10,000 miles. The two are correlated (0.63), so they share the work: R-squared of log price is 0.40 on age alone, 0.30 on miles alone and 0.44 on both. Across the 12 most listed brands the yearly loss runs from 4.4% (jeep) to 8.4% (hyundai), the loss per 10,000 miles from 0.6% (ram) to 6.4% (bmw). These are associations in what sellers list, not causes.

Each added fact helps, with diminishing returns. On one listing per car, with folds by car, the table gives the R-squared of log price, the typical miss (median absolute percentage error) and the share of cars predicted within 20% of the asking price. Age and miles alone leave a 35% miss; the make and engine take it to 23%, the make-model to 21% and boosting to 15%, a mean absolute error of $3,151.

How it is scored changes the answer. The same boosted model, in the table by how the folds are cut: random listings let a car's twins sit on both sides (52% of listings have a twin in another fold); folds by car stop that; one listing per car also stops repeated cars counting several times; unseen make-model holds out whole models; later cars trains on cars first posted up to 2021-04-26 and tests on the 91,056 cars first posted later. R-squared falls from 0.84 to 0.73 on an unseen model. The gap between random and by-car folds is modest for this model, whose settings were not varied.

What matters. Shuffling a feature inside each test fold and measuring the rise in out-of-fold error: age 0.34, make and model together 0.19, mileage 0.14, the engine fields 0.12, state 0.07, body type 0.04 and condition 0.04 (RMSE of log price). Age and mileage are correlated, so these are not additive shares.

An honest 90% range is wide. From each fold's neighbours' residuals the interval runs from 0.60 times to 1.66 times the prediction (40% below to 66% above): $11,813 wide for the median car, against $14,774 for the ridge fit and $23,649 for age and miles alone. Overall it covers 90.0% of cars, but not evenly: 81% under $5k and 95% above $40k. A range calibrated on random-fold residuals covers only 85.3% of new cars, and the correct one 86.9% of cars from unseen make-models.

05 — What I'd do next

  • Vary the boosting settings and a nearest-neighbour model to see whether a more flexible model exploits the twins more.
  • Separate dealers from private sellers (the file has no seller type) and see whether the 90% range narrows for either.
  • Repeat on another month or region to see whether the depreciation rates and the width of the range hold.
