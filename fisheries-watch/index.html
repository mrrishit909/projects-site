Built from a written blueprint as one vertical slice. The sea is simulated: the region, the fleet, its tracks, the AIS reception, the radar passes, the registry and who broke the rules come from a generator in the repository. No real vessel, MMSI, AIS, registry or satellite data is used. The detectors see only what a centre would; the truth is used to train on past cases from other simulated fortnights and to score. The simulated rule-breakers behave more distinctly than real ones, so every number is a best case. On the demo's last day the satellite passes are aimed at the reserve and the carrier, so the demo boat is always detected; the held-out evaluation does not force this. No language model is used. Not built: a trajectory sequence model, a SAR image detector, real feeds, Kafka, PostGIS, Terraform, single sign-on.

01The problem

Boats that fish where they should not usually switch their AIS transponder off first. But every boat goes quiet sometimes: offshore reception is patchy, some areas are barely covered, and transponders fail on their own. A rule like "flag every silence over six hours" catches the deliberate ones and buries them under a dozen honest ones a day. Radar satellites see boats with AIS off, but they also see waves, and a radar return has no name on it. Carriers that take catch off fishing boats at sea mostly wait for honest reasons. Can a centre tell which silences were on purpose, who a dark radar target could have been, which carrier met someone, and which boats to look at first, and say honestly how often it is wrong?

Blueprint 15 of twenty in a fourth build book, built as its demo scenario end to end.

02What I built

A Python service on PostgreSQL with a background worker and a web page, started by one docker compose up.

  • A simulated fortnight: 110 fishing boats, four reefer carriers and eight cargo ships at 10-minute steps, AIS that reaches the ground 92% of the time near the coast and 12% in the east, transponder failures, two radar passes a day with misses, position error and wave clutter, and about a dozen rule-breakers.
  • Coverage learned from the data and every silence of two hours or more scored by a logistic model on how improbable it was there, how near the reserve, how little ground it covered and whether a carrier was within reach.
  • Fusion of radar targets with AIS, and the silent boats that could have been each unmatched one.
  • Meetings: carrier encounters on AIS, and loitering carriers with no AIS partner.
  • A risk score per boat with each factor's contribution, and cases that freeze the evidence and need a second person to act.

03How it was built

  1. 01

    A sea where every boat goes quiet

    A 400 x 300 km region off a coast with two ports and the Coral Bank Marine Protected Area over the best fishing ground. Boats fish four grounds, one beside the reserve and one in the poorly covered east, and lawful ones turn back 1.5 km before the reserve's edge. About a dozen rule-breakers switch AIS off 8 to 45 km out and fish inside, or meet a carrier at sea; four honest boats transship with a declared authorisation. Every message then faces the reception lottery, and 4.5% of boat-days have a transponder failure of 3 to 16 hours. Over twelve days 82 fishing boats crossed the reserve on AIS and none was seen there at fishing speed.

    rec = (~tr.dark) & (~outage) & (~tr.away) & (r.random(SLOTS) < reception(tr.pos[:, 0], tr.pos[:, 1]))
  2. 02

    How unlikely was this silence, here?

    Coverage per 20 km cell is learned from the fixes themselves: received over expected messages, from silences short enough to be accidents. A silence is then scored on the log-probability of missing that many messages along its path, its length, nearness to and crossing of the reserve, the knots it implies, whether a loitering carrier was reachable, and whether it ends at the region's edge. The weights come from past cases in three other simulated fortnights: 1,680 silences, 48 of them deliberate.

    logp = float(np.log10(np.clip(1 - r, 1e-6, 1)).sum())   # r: coverage of each cell along the silence
  3. 03

    A radar return has no name

    Radar detections are matched to where AIS says ships were by the Hungarian algorithm, gated at 3 km and on length, so a 20 m return beside a 140 m carrier is not mistaken for the carrier. What is left over and 18 m or longer is a dark target. Its candidates are the boats silent at that moment that could have reached it at 15 knots from their last fix and back to their next, ranked by how suspicious their silence already looked. In the demo Southern Star 7 was silent for 22.3 hours, and the target inside the reserve could have been any of three boats, and the ranking puts the wrong one first. Both radar sightings of it are arranged: on the demo's last day the passes are aimed at the reserve and the carrier and always detect it, which the held-out evaluation does not do.

    if math.hypot(det["x"] - a[0], det["y"] - a[1]) > 15 * W.KM_PER_SLOT_PER_KN * (s - slots[j - 1]) + 2:
    continue
  4. 04

    Carriers that wait, and carriers that load

    An AIS encounter is a carrier and a fishing boat both under 2 knots within 1 km for two hours, over 20 km from port; declared transshipments are set aside. A carrier loitering for three hours with no AIS partner is a possible dark meeting, and its partners are silent boats that could have been there in its last two hours, because a carrier leaves when loading is done. In the demo, carriers loitered five times in 48 hours with no partner; once, Polar Crown had a 20 m radar target alongside that only Southern Star 7 could have been. The distance-only rule raised 21 meetings, led by two pair trawlers fishing together.

    if reachable(g, lx, ly, max(l0, l1 - 12), l1):
    partners.append((g.get("p", 0.0), g["vessel"]))
  5. 05

    A ranking that shows its working, and a case that needs two people

    Every fishing boat is scored over the fortnight by a logistic model on its worst silence, dark targets near the reserve attributed to it, undeclared encounters, being a loitering carrier's likely partner and the registry, and each factor's pull on the log-odds is shown. Southern Star 7 ranks 5th of 110; the top ten holds 7 of the 12 rule-breakers, against 3 for a ranking by silent hours. The analyst opens a case that freezes the evidence with its SHA-256; the same analyst cannot send a patrol, a supervisor does, and every step is in a hash-chained audit log.

    if k["opened_by"] == ctx.actor_id:
    raise Problem(409, "four_eyes", "the analyst who opened the case cannot escalate it")

What these say plainly. The gap model's value is workload: nearly the six-hour rule's recall with a sixth of the alerts, but about half its alerts are honest boats whose transponders failed near the reserve. Radar alone rarely names a boat: most dark targets have several silent candidates, and of 7–10 deliberately dark boats seen per fortnight the right one is named first for 5–6. Most loitering carriers are only waiting. The ranking is good because simulated rule-breakers do several distinctive things at once; real ones are fewer, quieter and better at it.

Checks. 10 tests, including: a boat sends nothing while dark, lawful boats never fish inside the reserve, coverage learned from the data separates coast from east, reachability in time and distance, the detectors beat the simple rules on an unseen fortnight, fusion matches AIS correctly and leaves the dark boat unmatched even beside a carrier, the whole demo against a real database, spoofed fixes flagged, the four-eyes rule, and one centre cannot see another's data.

05What I'd do next

  • Use the radar's misses as evidence too: a boat whose last fix puts it inside a swath where nothing was detected was probably elsewhere.
  • Learn coverage per hour and per vessel, so a transponder that fails often stops looking suspicious.
  • Labels from real past investigations, which are fewer and biased towards what was already looked for, and an evaluation that says how much that costs.
