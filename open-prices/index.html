Does One Barcode Cost the Same Everywhere?

Volunteers' prices for one barcode differ by a median 12% between the dearest and cheapest French shop (46% at the 90th percentile, 17,488 cases); the shop's price level explains 60%. Across barcodes pack size adds only 3.1% to category's 43.5%.

Python · pandas · Sparse least squares · Fixed effects · Cluster-robust errors · Open Prices, Open Food Facts (ODbL 1.0) · Eurostat HICP · Hand-written recomputeCode on GitHub ↗

Prices are entered by volunteers who choose what, where and when (top 1% of contributors: 90%; 67% French), from photos of tags and receipts, with discounts; no result is representative of any country's shelves. Data: Open Prices, Open Food Facts contributors, ODbL 1.0 (only this project's aggregates are published, shared alike); Eurostat HICP France (ends December 2025). Retailers were not scraped.

01 — The problem

Volunteers photograph price tags and receipts into a free database. Does one barcode cost the same everywhere, and how much of the difference is the shop, the pack size or a mistake?

The task follows GeeksforGeeks' "Product Price Scraping and Analysis". Scraping retailers is not allowed here, so the data is the compliant, real stand-in: Open Food Facts' crowdsourced Open Prices database (ODbL), from its official bulk file, with Eurostat's French consumer price index as the official benchmark.

02 — What I built

One bulk file, audited and then asked five questions:

  • The audit: a cleaning funnel and every trap counted: duplicates, units, currencies, outliers, loyalty-card exports.
  • The gap: the same barcode in different French shops, five ways of cleaning, and other countries in their own currencies.
  • The shop and the size: how much of the gap is the shop's price level, and how little of a price across barcodes is pack size.
  • Receipts and inflation: receipts against price tags, and a year of repeat prices against Eurostat.
  • Check: check.py recounts all 653 numbers in plain Python, solving the fits a different way.

03 — How it was built

  1. 01

    Download the bulk file, not the shelves

    The official daily Open Prices export (320,399 prices), the Open Food Facts product table streamed and filtered to the priced barcodes (116,467), and Eurostat's French HICP; about 49 MB stored, never committed, with checksums. No retailer site is touched.

    PRICES_URL = "https://huggingface.co/datasets/openfoodfacts/open-prices/resolve/main/prices.parquet"
  2. 02

    Audit, then flag the outliers

    A cleaning funnel from 320,399 rows to 291,812, every trap counted, and a far outlier defined as 3 times or a third of the barcode's median, for barcodes seen 5 or more times.

    A["far"] = (A["bn"] >= 5) & ((A["ratio"] >= FAR) | (A["ratio"] <= 1 / FAR))
  3. 03

    Measure the gap between shops

    One value per barcode, shop and quarter (mean log price); a case is a barcode sold in 2 or more shops in a quarter, and its gap is the dearest over the cheapest, minus 1.

    sp = np.expm1(g.max() - g.min()); sd = g.std(ddof=1); ns = g.size()
  4. 04

    Take out the shop's price level

    Log price is fitted as a case effect plus a shop effect, by sparse least squares in the largest connected set of shops; the share of the within-case variance the shop effect removes, and the chain levels, come from it.

    sol = lsqr(X, y, atol=1e-14, btol=1e-14, iter_lim=20000, conlim=1e12)[0]
  5. 05

    Recount it all without the libraries

    check.py reads the files with the csv and gzip modules and recomputes every table, solving the same fits by alternating projections instead: 653 numbers, at 1e-9 for counts and shares and 1e-6 for the fitted effects.

    [(res, acc)] = ap([y], [cc_, ss_]); rss = sum(e * e for e in res); seff = [acc[1][ss_[i]] for i in range(n)]; m0 = mean(seff); seff = [x - m0 for x in seff]

04 — Results

Who the crowd is. 320,399 prices, 140,875 barcodes, 6,977 shops, 3,659 contributors; the top 37 contributors (1%) entered 90% of them, and the top 10 shops 25%. France is 67% of the cleaned prices, supermarkets 84% of all rows, and 2026-02 alone 34,233 prices, 73% of them from one contributor.

The file is mostly clean. 291,812 rows (91%) survive the cleaning funnel. Only 36% of the cleaned rows belong to a barcode seen 5 or more times, the only ones whose outliers can be seen: 104 of those prices are 3 times or a third of the barcode's median, 2 of them decimal slips. The shares have different bases: all rows for raw produce, loyalty exports and old dates; the rows reaching the step for duplicates and currencies; the discounted rows for the missing regular price; the checkable rows for far outliers and the cleaned rows for the checkable ones.

Across barcodes, pack size explains little. Within 156 categories (10,440 barcodes), category explains 43.5% of log price, pack size adds 3.1% (price rises 0.17 per unit of log size, not 1), the shop 8.2%; 45% is left to brand, quality and pack type. 05 — What I'd do next

  • Add a second country with enough prices (Norway has 18 thousand) and compare it with its own official price index.
  • Separate chain from shop format with OpenStreetMap shop tags, and test whether chain levels are stable from one quarter to the next.
  • Rerun as the crowd grows: the repeat-price comparison needs many more shops revisited before it can confirm or contradict an official index.
