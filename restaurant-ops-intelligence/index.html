What Should 50 Kitchens Prep When the Truck Is Late and It Rains?

Operations intelligence for an invented 50-store chain: Monday's lunch rush forecast item by item every 15 minutes, a late produce truck and a lunchtime rainstorm re-planned the night before, and the same three days replayed: 1.9% of demand lost to stockouts and $37.4k thrown away, against 9.5% and $48.6k on the plan made before the notices and 13.0% and $56.6k on the chain's usual practice.

Built from a written blueprint as one vertical slice. The chain is simulated: stores, customers, kitchens, suppliers, weather and walk-ins come from a generator in the repository, and the late truck and the storm are injected by telling the generator about them, which is what lets every forecast, plan and alarm be checked against the truth. The replay re-runs the same generator, so its waste and lost sales are as right as the simulator. No real restaurant data. Not built: LightGBM or PyTorch forecasters, a labor scheduler, store-to-store transfers, real POS, supplier and weather connectors, Kafka, ClickHouse, Kubernetes, Terraform, single sign-on.

01 / The problem

A restaurant chain decides twice a day, in every store, how much chicken to grill and rice to cook before the rush, and every night how much lettuce, beef and avocado to order for deliveries two days out. Most chains decide from a par sheet: what the same day sold last week, plus a margin. That margin is thrown away on quiet days and runs short on busy ones, it knows nothing about the rain or the promotion, and it learns from sales, so a store that ran out last Wednesday orders less for this one. When a supplier calls to say the truck will be a day late, the par sheet has no answer at all. Can software forecast every store's demand finely enough to prep from, carry the uncertainty into prep and orders, re-plan the night a truck is late and a storm is coming, leave the bigger order changes to a store manager, and show what the re-plan saved?

Blueprint 10 of twenty in a fourth build book, built as its demo scenario end to end.

02 / What I built

A Python service on PostgreSQL with a background worker and a web page, started by one docker compose up.

  • A simulated chain: 50 stores in three formats, a 12-item menu built from 15 ingredients and 6 prepped components, two sales channels, rain, local events, promotions with a true elasticity per item, a kitchen that preps, tops up and throws away, lots that spoil faster in a warm walk-in, three suppliers, and four stores losing stock to theft or a heavy hand.
  • A 15-minute hierarchical forecast with intervals at every level, fitted only on slots where the item was on sale.
  • Prep plans to the forecast's 90th percentile, with labor and a crew curve, and orders to the 95th, net of the stock a spoilage hazard expects to survive, with a backup order when a truck is late.
  • Order changes beyond $250 a line go to a store manager, never the person who asked.
  • A replay of the same days under other plans, a food-cost bridge from recipe to actual, and shrinkage detection against the chain's normal.

03 / How it was built

  1. 01

    A chain whose customers the kitchen never fully sees

    Demand is generated per store, item, channel and 15-minute slot from the store's volume and format, its hour shape and item mix, the rain (dine-in falls by half per unit of intensity, delivery rises 60%), events, promotions and a shared shock per store and day. When a component runs out the kitchen cooks a top-up that takes half an hour; an item that cannot be made is 86'd and the customer is lost, so the sales understate demand exactly when it was highest. Every random draw is keyed by day and purpose, so the same days can be replayed under another plan with the same customers. Eight weeks of history ran the chain's usual way and threw away 12.5% of $6.37M of purchases.

    def typical(self, hist, ran_out, t):
    """The same weekday over the last two weeks: their mean, or, if either ran out, the larger, with 10% more."""
    a, b = hist[t - 7], hist[t - 14]
    out = ran_out[t - 7] | ran_out[t - 14]
    return np.where(out, self.BUMP * np.maximum(a, b), (a + b) / 2)
  2. 02

    A forecast that adds up and says how sure it is

    Each store's hour shape is shrunk toward its format's and its item mix toward the chain's, so a slot with three sales borrows strength from the stores like it; rain, events and each item's elasticity are fitted on top, on the slots where the item was on sale. A store-day shock and slot-level overdispersion give intervals at every level, which is why the chain's interval is narrow and a store's is not. On three held-out chains the 15-minute WAPE was 0.485-0.508 against 0.670-0.700 for the same slot last week, and the 90% interval held 91.0-91.2% of item lunch rushes, inside the blueprint's 85-95% band. A refresh of all 57,600 slots took 711 ms at p95 inside the job.

    s_ = (n_ + KAPPA_SHAPE * prior) / (d_ + KAPPA_SHAPE)   # a store's hour shape, shrunk toward its format's
shape[:, :, t, :] = s_ * S / s_.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
  3. 03

    Prep to the day's own risk, and update at three

    Each component is prepped to the 90th percentile of its window's demand rather than to last week plus 15%. At 15:00 the dinner prep is re-planned from what lunch actually sold: a busy lunch says the day's shock is up, and the dinner quantity moves with it. Over 14 days on three held-out chains, waste fell from 12.1% of purchases to 9.0% and lost demand from 3.10% to 1.93%, with the planner running unattended; $135k of the $156k of waste avoided was prep.

    k = 1 / self.m.sigma2
f = (y + k) / (m_ + k)     # lunch sold against lunch forecast: the day's shock, shrunk toward 1
  4. 04

    A late truck needs one decision

    When the supplier's notice arrives, tonight's orders are re-run with the delivery where it will really land. Stores that would run out of produce before the truck arrives get a backup order from the cash-and-carry at a 35% premium. In the demo that was 20 stores and $11.2k of produce, and it is most of what the re-plan bought: on the first day the plan made before the notices lost 25.8% of demand, barely better than usual practice's 27.1%, while the re-plan lost 2.2%.

    short = need_m + _z(self.q_ing) * np.sqrt(need_v) - stock - arriving
big = short > np.maximum(0.15 * need_m, 0.5 * W.CASE)
q = np.where(big & (late > 0) | big & (short > 0.5 * need_m), W.round_cases(short), 0)
  5. 05

    A store manager signs the big changes

    Lines within $250 of what the usual par would order are placed automatically; larger changes are a proposal. The planner who asked for the recommendation cannot approve it, and a store manager who asked for one cannot approve their own. Running unattended inside a day, the planner places the usual par for any line beyond the limit, which gives back a little of each gain (9.0% waste against 8.8% with every line approved).

    if d["proposed_by"] == ctx.actor_id:
    raise Problem(403, "proposer_cannot_approve", "order changes above the limit need a second person")
  6. 06

    Shrinkage against the chain, not a threshold

    Counted usage is compared with sales at recipe per store and ingredient, and judged against the chain's median for that ingredient. The chain's avocados yield less flesh than the recipe assumes everywhere, so a fixed 4% variance rule flags every store; against the chain's normal, all 24 injected cases on six held-out chains were found, none falsely, and each was classed correctly as theft or over-portioning.

    norm = np.median(v, 0)                 # the chain's normal variance, per ingredient
excess = v - norm
z = excess / np.maximum(se, 1e-9)

Honest limits. The spoilage hazard ranks lots better than age or the printed date but is no better in ordering: with printed dates instead the planner wasted $401,862 against $402,804 over 14 days, because lots here turn over in two or three days. At 15 minutes a literal 90% interval covers 96%, because an item sells about three units a slot and the interval's ends are whole numbers; the randomised PIT puts 89.7–89.9% inside it. The weather forecast the re-plan saw is the generator's own storm, so it was nearly perfect. The forecast is scored on true demand, which no real chain has, and it trusts the kitchen to flag every item it 86's.

Checks. 13 tests, including: another plan meets the same customers, rain and promotions move demand by the generator's numbers, the kitchen never sells more than was demanded and flags every slot it sold short, the forecast recovers the rain, event and elasticity effects and its store-day interval holds on the next week, the hazard learns that a warm walk-in spoils lettuce, shrinkage flags exactly the injected stores while the threshold flags the chain, the planner beats usual practice and backs up a late truck mostly where it is late, the replay reproduces the run, a proposer cannot approve, and one restaurant group cannot see another's sales. The Docker stack was run end to end.
