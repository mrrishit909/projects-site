Built from a written blueprint as one vertical slice. Everything is simulated: the territory, the 10,000 devices, their owners' behaviour, the weather, sixty days of telemetry and the market come from a generator in the repository. Nothing is a real utility, aggregator, customer or market. The models learn only from the telemetry (with its noise, gaps and stale registry entries) and are scored against the hidden truth on three territories the demo never uses; where they share a functional form with the generator, their accuracy is the best case. No language model is used. Not built: real protocols (OpenADR, OCPP), a real market's tariff, stochastic dispatch with recourse, per-device data in a time-series store, Kafka, SSO.

01The problem

An aggregator sells a fleet of home devices to the grid as if it were a power plant: so many megawatts for three hours on a hot evening. The registry says what every thermostat, charger and battery is rated for, but on the night owners give up, cars are not plugged in, batteries are half worn and keep a reserve nobody told the registry about, and the load comes back when the event ends. Bid too much and the penalties eat the payment; bid too little and the fleet is idle. The market then pays on a baseline that is its own estimate of what the homes would have used. Can a platform learn what the fleet will really give, bid it, dispatch it without overloading a feeder, survive losing contact with part of it, and say honestly how the money came out?

Blueprint 17 of twenty in a fourth build book, built as its demo scenario end to end.

02What I built

A Python service on PostgreSQL with a background worker and a web page, started by one docker compose up.

  • A simulated territory: 10,000 homes on eight feeders, a summer of weather, telemetry with its flaws, eight past events.
  • Availability models per device, learned from telemetry and back-tested on the past events.
  • A deliverable distribution and a bid at its 25th percentile, with a policy check.
  • A dispatch linear programme over device groups that holds the bid and every feeder limit through the rebound.
  • Edge schedules that keep running when a gateway drops, and a re-plan from what the cloud can still see.
  • Settlement on four baselines, scored against the simulation's counterfactual.

03How it was built

  1. 01

    A fleet that is not what its registry says

    4,620 thermostats, 1,934 EV chargers, 1,784 batteries and 1,662 solar roofs: 41.9 MW of flexible nameplate on eight feeders. Hidden from the platform: how hard each air conditioner works (it tops out at its rating), how quickly its owner gives up as the heat builds, how often each car is plugged in at 17:00, each battery's real state of health and the reserve its owner really keeps, which is stale in the registry for one battery in six. Sixty days of feeder and meter telemetry come with 680 missing readings. A second file is checked row by row: a pool pump, a 30 kW 'home' battery and a feeder that does not exist are refused.

    if not lo <= r.nameplate_kw <= hi:
    return f"{r.nameplate_kw} kW is outside {lo}-{hi} kW for a residential {r.kind}"
  2. 02

    Learning what the devices really do

    From eight past events and sixty days: setpoint cut 44.9% (truth 45.0%), EV override 8.2% (8.0%), the give-up hazard by discrete-time maximum likelihood with a frailty per home. Battery capacity from kWh delivered per point of charge: 19.2 MWh against 22.5 on the nameplate (truth 19.2), and all 68 stale reserves found from where batteries actually stopped. The air-conditioning fits are straight lines in temperature from days that never reached this heat: capped at the registered rating they say 16.8 MW at 18:00 (truth 16.7); uncapped, 19.7. Back-tested on the past events the fleet-level prediction ran 1.9% high every time, so it is scaled down by that.

    hz = np.clip(np.exp(th[0] + th[1] * (temp - 32)), 1e-9, 1 - 1e-9)
return -(gave_up * np.log(hz) + (at_risk - gave_up) * np.log1p(-hz)).sum()
  3. 03

    A forecast past the edge of the history, and a bid

    Tomorrow: 38.3 °C forecast, hotter than any of the sixty days. The regression, trained only on ordinary days (the hottest days all had events), puts the system peak at 111.0 MW against a true 109.1; the 10-in-10 average says 85.7. Every feeder is forecast over its limit. The fleet's deliverable, held flat 17:00-20:00 over 300 draws: P10 12.8, P50 13.4, P90 13.9 MW, a third of nameplate. The bid is the 25th percentile, 13.1 MW, where one more megawatt's capacity payment equals its expected /MWh penalty. A nameplate bid is refused; the industry's performance factor would have bid 6.4.

    Q_BID = PRICES["capacity"] / PRICES["penalty"]      # newsvendor quantile
if body.mw > d["p50"] and not body.override_reason: raise Problem(409, "bid_exceeds_deliverable")
  4. 04

    A plan that survives the rebound

    A linear programme per feeder over six thermostat cohorts, charger throttling and batteries in three health tiers (worn ones cost more per kWh: /bin/zsh.25, /bin/zsh.32, /bin/zsh.41): hold 13.1 MW every 15 minutes net of rebound, keep every feeder under its limit until 23:00, release over two hours. It costs ,108 and spreads 11.8 of 12.7 MWh of battery to rise as owners give up. It cannot keep F5 and F8 fully inside their limits at the peak, so it will not run until a person accepts that. On the true day it held 13.0 / 13.0 / 12.8 MW with every feeder inside its limit; calling everything at once would have held 17.6 / 14.1 / 8.6 and pushed F4 0.39 MW over on the restart.

  5. 05

    A feeder goes dark, and the market pays on its own baseline

    8,321 schedules go out at 16:45. At 18:15 feeder F4's gateway drops: 1,476 devices the cloud cannot see. They keep their schedules, and the evening comes out the same as with no outage (38.8 MWh). Had they reverted, 35.7 MWh, F4 1.25 MW over its limit, and the re-plan could find only 0.39 MW of spare battery. Settlement: 38.7 MWh truly delivered; the market's 10-in-10 baseline, its day-of adjustment capped at 20% on a heat wave, credits 27.8 MWh and pays ,807 net after ,219 of penalties on hours the fleet met.

The deliverable model is a few percent optimistic and its interval too narrow: ten thousand devices average out, so only what they share is left in the spread, and the truth fell below its P10 on two of three territories. Even bidding the 25th percentile fell slightly short in the last hour every time. The market's baseline decides more of the money than the fleet does.

Honest limits. Everything is simulated, and the models share some functional forms with the generator; the edge fallback's 'no loss' is a design the simulator implements, so it is the best case. One event window on one day.

Checks. 8 tests, including: the day with no control equals the counterfactual, batteries never go below their true reserve and deferred charging is moved not lost, devices that keep their schedule lose nothing while reverting ones do and the re-plan covers part of it, calibration recovers the hidden behaviour and finds the stale reserves, the plan holds the bid inside its own model with every battery under 5 kW, settlement arithmetic, the whole demo through the API with policy refusals and idempotent replay, and one aggregator cannot see another's fleet.

05What I'd do next

  • Put the models' structural error into the deliverable distribution (a back-test of the plan, not just of each model), so the bid stops falling short in the last hour.
  • Two-stage dispatch over the deliverable draws instead of planning on expected availability.
  • Per-device telemetry in a time-series store and fitted parameters in object storage, so the worker does not regenerate them.
