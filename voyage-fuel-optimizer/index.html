Through the Storm, or Around It?

Voyage optimisation for an invented North Atlantic container line: a crossing to Rotterdam is routed and speeded against its berth window, a storm and a full port appear two days out, the operator compares four alternatives, a second person approves the re-route, and she arrives inside the approved band having burned 211 t (39%) less fuel and cost $157,222 (22%) less than the same voyage at service speed through the same weather.

Built from a written blueprint as one vertical slice. The ocean and the fleet are simulated: the weather, storms and currents, six ships with their own speed-power curves and fouling hulls, and every noon report and AIS position come from a generator in the repository. The demo's storm and port congestion are injected by telling the generator; the planners only see forecasts, port line-ups and the ships' reports. Savings are measured against a baseline the simulator re-sails, so they are as right as its physics. No real vessel, AIS or weather data. Not built: real weather grids, PostGIS, a learned speed-power model, Kafka, Kubernetes, Terraform, single sign-on.

01 / The problem

A container ship crossing the Atlantic has a berth window at the other end, a forecast that is good for a few days, and a fuel bill that grows with the cube of her speed. Sailing at service speed and waiting at anchor is the habit, and it is expensive; slowing down saves fuel until a storm or a full port moves the arrival, and then every change of plan is a change of the ETA the charterer was given. Can software set the route and the speed for the weather and the window, say how sure it is of the arrival, tell the operator in time when a storm or the port changes the picture, put the alternatives side by side with their fuel, CO2 and penalties, keep a second person on any change beyond the charter terms, and prove what it saved against the plain way through the same weather?

Blueprint 9 of twenty in a fourth build book, built as its demo scenario end to end.

02 / What I built

A Python service on PostgreSQL with a background worker and a web page, started by one docker compose up.

  • A simulated North Atlantic: a 0.5-degree ocean with westerlies, travelling storms and the Gulf Stream; forecasts whose errors grow with lead time; six ships with their own true fuel curves, fouling hulls and noon reports.
  • A fuel model per ship fitted on its noon reports, with the hull-fouling penalty it implies.
  • Weather routing by time-dependent A* on the forecast, and speed orders by dynamic programming against the berth window, with an ETA band from a 24-member ensemble.
  • A voyage monitor that raises storms on the track and port congestion, alternatives compared side by side, and approval by a second person for changes beyond the charter terms.
  • A bunker plan (MILP) and a voyage report against the weather-normalised baseline.

03 / How it was built

  1. 01

    An ocean whose forecasts are honestly wrong

    The weather is a function of time: westerlies with travelling meanders, storms that form off North America and run east-north-east, waves that follow the wind. A forecast is the truth with errors that grow with lead time, and it cannot contain storms that form more than two days after it is issued. Ensemble members carry storms of their own beyond that horizon, drawn from the same climatology, so the real weather is statistically one more member. That is what makes an ETA band computable and checkable.

    if self.member and include_climatology:
    alt = (self.seed * 7919 + int(round(self.issued)) * 104729 + self.member * 15485863) % (2 ** 61)
    out += [dict(s, id=f"m{self.member}:{s['id']}") for s in storms_for(alt, lo_t, hi_t) if s["t0"] > cutoff]
  2. 02

    A speed order is an engine setting

    The first version held speed through the water: weather showed up as extra fuel, and the ETA band came out minutes wide while real arrivals missed it by hours. Ships run on an engine setting, so here an order is the power that gives that speed in calm water, and waves and wind cost time instead. Each ship's fuel model is fitted on its noon reports by non-negative least squares on physically shaped terms. On held-out worlds it was within 2.0-3.5% a day, against 3.5-17.0% for the yard's sea-trial curve, and it read the hull's fouling to within 0.9 points at the median.

    target = np.minimum(power(v, cmd, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, disp, days_clean), 0.9 * v["mcr_kw"])
for _ in range(5):
    stw = stw * (target / power(v, stw, hs, g, head, disp, days_clean)) ** (1 / 3)
  3. 03

    Routing that knows when not to route

    Time-dependent A* searches 7,283 ocean nodes with the forecast at the hour the ship would reach each one, then smooths the path into great-circle legs. Both it and the shortest sea route are then sailed through the forecast, and the cheaper is kept. At the demo's departure the forecast gave no reason to leave the great circle, so the shortest route won. Solves took a median 1.7 s and at worst 3.0 s against a 30-second bar.

    best = "optimized" if evals["optimized"]["cost"] <= evals["shortest"]["cost"] else "shortest"
  4. 04

    A plan that keeps its P90 inside the window

    Dynamic programming picks an order for every leg of about 120 miles, weighing fuel and carbon against hire, waiting at anchor and the late penalty. The plan is then sailed through the ensemble; if its P90 arrival is after the window, it tightens its own deadline and plans again. In the demo the first try's P90 was almost six hours late; the second landed 9 minutes inside. On held-out voyages the realised arrival was at or before the P90 89% of the time.

    if speed != "optimize" or p90 <= limit + 0.5:
    break
dl = (dl if dl is not None else sp["arrival_h"]) - (p90 - limit) - 0.5
  5. 05

    A change of ETA needs a second person

    Two days out, the monitor's fresh forecast puts 7.1 m seas on her track and Rotterdam's line-up has 17 ships at anchor. Four alternatives go side by side. Keeping the plan sails into 10.4 m seas to wait 25 hours. Holding the ETA given to the charterer waits 36. Re-routing south of the storm and slowing to the berth is the cheapest at $452,977, but it moves the ETA 31 hours, beyond the 12 the charter allows, so it becomes a proposal. The operator who proposed it cannot approve it; the master does.

    if d["proposed_by"] == ctx.actor_id:
    raise Problem(403, "proposer_cannot_approve", "a plan change beyond the charter terms needs a second person")
  6. 06

    Measured against the plain way, through the same weather

    At berthing the voyage report re-sails the baseline from the same departure: the shortest route at service speed, through the same weather, storm included, then waiting for the berth. A real deployment can only do that with the fitted model on reanalysis weather; a simulator can also re-sail its own ship, and the report shows both. The late penalty never counts waiting that the port caused.

    late = np.maximum(0.0, arrival_h - max(terms["window_close_h"], berth_h))

The bunker MILP saved $1,276,001 over 36 plans against topping up at every call (0.7% at the median).

Honest limits. Almost all the saving is speed: against a master who picks one sensible speed for the window, the optimiser saves 1.7% and was dearer on 9 of 36 voyages. Weather routing alone, at service speed, was 0.34% dearer on average in ordinary weather; it pays in storms, in sea not met rather than money (re-routing cost 0.4% more than keeping the plan). The P90 is calibrated, but a fixed six-hour margin covered as often on ordinary voyages; the P50 is a little early (39%). An automatic re-speed within the charter terms was built and removed, because it chased each new forecast. The demo's world was chosen so the injected storm is the only severe weather on the crossing.

Checks. 13 tests, including: forecast errors grow with lead time and a storm that forms after the horizon is invisible, an engine setting makes less speed into head seas than following ones, the fuel model beats the sea-trial curve and finds the fouling, the route goes around a storm the great circle sails into, the speed plan keeps its deadline and beats service speed, the bunker MILP is never worse than topping up, an implausible noon report is refused with the reason, the proposer cannot approve, and one operator cannot see another's fleet, through the API or in the database. The Docker stack was run end to end.

05 / What I'd do next

  • Real forecasts and ensembles (GRIB through xarray) and real noon reports, where the fuel model meets reporting habits this simulator does not have.
  • Re-planning every day that does not chase the forecast: a band on how far a plan may move before it is worth changing.
  • Fleet-level scheduling, so that one ship's slow steaming and another's bunker stop are planned together.
