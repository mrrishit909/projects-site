How Many Cycles Does This Engine Have Left?

01 / The problem

What needed answering

An engine does not fail on the day its fault code fires; it has been wearing for hundreds of cycles, and the question for maintenance control is how many it has left, with what confidence, and whether there is a hangar night before then where the aircraft happens to be and a spare module happens to exist. The fleet's reliability curve answers for the population; the engine's own trend answers for the engine; a mechanic's finding is the only thing that tells you which was right. Can software estimate remaining life honestly, plan a removal that avoids an aircraft on the ground, and keep every recommendation traceable to its evidence?

Blueprint 12 of fifteen in a third build book, built as its demo scenario end to end.

02 / What was built

The build

A Python service on PostgreSQL with a background worker and a web page, started by one docker compose up.

  • Ingestion of takeoff telemetry and fault codes, with ranges, duplicates and attachment to the right engine.
  • Health and anomaly: a smoothed EGT-margin trend and a PCA reconstruction score, labelled a stand-in for the blueprint's autoencoder.
  • Remaining life from a line over three windows with a bootstrap band, the fleet Weibull beside it, the prediction stored with its evidence.
  • A planner (CP-SAT) over hangar nights, where each aircraft sleeps and modules in stock, minimising AOG risk plus life given up.
  • Feedback: the finding closes the work order, swaps the module and refits the Weibull.
03 / How it was done

Step by step

  1. Step 01

    A fleet that wears out the way the data says

    Each HPC module's EGT margin falls as a power of cycles over its own life, slowly then faster, after the C-MAPSS pattern; vibration, fuel flow and oil pressure follow the wear with noise; fault codes fire on fixed thresholds and one in seven is spurious; when the margin is gone the module is removed unscheduled and a fresh one goes on. Forty aircraft fly a weekly pattern through a hub for six months: 23,008 flights, 276,096 telemetry values, two unscheduled removals.

    margin_true = EGT_MARGIN_NEW - (EGT_MARGIN_NEW - 2) * (on_wing / e["life"]) ** e["curve"]
  2. Step 02

    An anomaly score that admits what it is

    PCA with two components on four takeoff metrics, fitted on 37,000 flights whose smoothed margin was above 40; the reconstruction error over the healthy 99th percentile is the score, sustained when it stays above 1 for ten flights. The blueprint names an autoencoder; on four metrics the linear version does the same job, and the page says it is a stand-in. The evaluation finds the score fires about when the first genuine fault code does: in this world the margin is the signal, and the score's gain is fewer false alarms, not lead.

    rec = Z @ comps.T @ comps
score = np.sqrt(((Z - rec) ** 2).sum(axis=1)) / healthy_p99
  3. Step 03

    Remaining life: the engine's trend against the fleet's curve

    A line on the smoothed margin over the last 150, 300 and 450 cycles, projected to the removal threshold, with residual bootstraps pooled across the windows for a band: 68 cycles, 60 to 139. The fleet Weibull, a right-censored maximum-likelihood fit on two removals and eighty modules still on wing, says 4,567, because it knows the population and not the engine; the page says two events barely identify it. The truth is 43: outside the band, and the page says why: a line on a steepening curve is optimistic late in life, which is why the planner uses the P10.

    samples.append(horizon(np.polyfit(cc, yb, 1)))   # per window, per bootstrap
  4. Step 04

    A hangar night that comes before the failure

    For each engine whose P10 life falls inside 30 days, CP-SAT chooses a hangar night where the aircraft sleeps, or none, under slots per night (two at the hub, one in the north) and modules available by day (two in stock, one more after a ten-day lead). The objective is the AOG cost times the chance of failing before the slot plus the life given up, against the AOG cost times the chance of failing in the horizon. The demo's engine gets tomorrow night at the hub; the truth says it fails on day 13. The work order is a proposal until a person performs it.

    terms.append(int(aog_cost * p_before + early_cost_per_cycle * early) * v)
  5. Step 05

    The finding goes back into the record

    The mechanic records blade tip erosion confirmed. The module is swapped (the new serial installed, stock down to one), the proposal cannot be closed twice, and the chapter's Weibull is refitted with the finding counted as a degradation event, which a 'no fault found' would not be. The reliability view shows removals, MTBUR, findings and the survival curve, with the note that a handful of removals says little about the shape and the scale is the number to read.

    events = [unscheduled or finding.lower().startswith("hpc") for each removal] + [False] * on_wing

The anomaly score adds no lead over a fixed EGT threshold in this world, because the margin is the signal; its gain is fewer false alarms. The RUL line is close near failure and optimistic late, and its band is too narrow. Six failures across eight fleets are small numbers, and the evaluation says so first.

Honest limits. One failure mode on a smooth curve; takeoff summaries rather than flight data; a Weibull fitted on two or three events, shown as the baseline a desk without telemetry would have.

Checks. 11 tests, including: the true margin only falls while a module is on wing and the demo engine really fails in the window, the worn engine's health is low and its anomaly sustained while a healthy one's is not, the RUL band covers the truth at least half the time and is optimistic ten days out, the Weibull fit recovers a known shape and scale, the planner never books a night the aircraft is elsewhere and never exceeds slots or stock, a duplicate telemetry value and a future fault are refused, a work order cannot be closed twice, and one operator cannot see another's fleet.

05 / What comes next

Next

  • A second failure mode the margin does not show (bearing wear in vibration), which is where the population curve starts to earn its place beside the trend.
  • A wider band: a curve instead of a line, or a block bootstrap over the autocorrelated trend.
  • A real public engine data set (C-MAPSS), with the honest number that follows.
[ Note ]

Built from a written blueprint as one vertical slice. The fleet is simulated: 40 aircraft, 80 engine modules, six months of flights, telemetry, fault codes and maintenance history come from a generator in the repository with one failure mode (compressor wear on a smooth curve with noise), which is what lets every remaining-life estimate be checked against the truth. Nothing is a real aircraft or airworthiness advice; every removal is a proposal a person performs. No language model is used. Not built: flight-data decoding, the autoencoder (PCA stands in and says so), survival models with covariates, more failure modes, an M&E adapter, Kafka, Terraform, single sign-on.
