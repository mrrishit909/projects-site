Does ARIMA Beat 'Same Month Last Year'?

Before COVID, SARIMA cut the error of 'same month last year' by 36% for furniture stores (26% even 12 months ahead) but not at all for office and gift stores (-3%). In 2020-21 SARIMA's 80% range held in only 37% and 24% of months, and from 2022 last year's number won again.

01 / The problem

What needed answering

Furniture sells slowly through the year and rises with the economy; office supplies and gifts swing with the calendar. Does a proper time-series model (ARIMA, with the seasonality and the calendar handled) beat the simplest forecast, "same month last year", on months it had not seen, and what do its ranges promise?

The task follows DataCamp's "An End-to-End Project on Time Series Analysis and Forecasting with Python" (ARIMA on furniture and office supplies sales). The original uses the Tableau Superstore sample file, which is synthetic; this uses the real Census Bureau monthly sales of furniture stores and of office supplies, stationery and gift stores, 1992 to 2026, not seasonally adjusted so that the seasonality is visible.

02 / What was built

The build

Two real series, one time-series workflow:

  • Decomposition and tests: trend, seasonal index and strength, ADF and KPSS on the differences, and a trading-day regression.
  • A walk-forward test: 188 forecast origins from December 2009, eight models refitted at each, 12 months ahead: last year's number, trend regressions, ETS, SARIMA, SARIMA with trading days, and a version that skips the lockdown months.
  • Scores and tests: error against last year's number by horizon, Diebold-Mariano tests, and how often the 80% and 95% ranges held.
  • The COVID break: what the February 2020 forecast said about April 2020, and how the crash echoes a year later.
  • Check: check.py recounts all 47,425 numbers in plain Python, with no data, statistics or modelling library; the statsmodels forecasts are scored from the stored numbers.
03 / How it was done

Step by step

  1. Step 01

    Download the three Census series

    Three FRED series of Census Bureau monthly retail sales, not seasonally adjusted, January 1992 to July 2026 (about 20 KB, fetched and never committed, with checksums): furniture and home furnishings stores, office supplies, stationery and gift stores, and, only to see what that total is made of, office supplies and stationery stores alone. Public domain; FRED stalls on a custom request header, so the default one is used.

    url = f"https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id={s}"
  2. Step 02

    Decompose and test for stationarity

    A classical multiplicative decomposition (centred 12-month average, then month averages) gives the trend and the seasonal index. ADF and KPSS, each with 12 lags, are run on the log level and its first, seasonal and first-plus-seasonal differences, on the first training window only, to choose d and D.

    a = adfuller(z.values, maxlag=12, regression="c", autolag=None)[0]; k = kpss(z.values, regression="c", nlags=12)[0]
  3. Step 03

    Walk forward, refitting at every origin

    For each month from December 2009 to July 2025 every model is refitted on the months up to then and forecasts the next 12 (2,256 forecasts per model and series). Orders were chosen once, by AIC on 1992-2009. The regression benchmarks and last year's number are hand-coded; the SARIMA variants add Saturdays, Sundays and leap-February as regressors, and one treats March-June 2020 as missing.

    m = sarima_fit(hist, spec[0], spec[1], exog); f = m.get_forecast(H, exog=fex)
  4. Step 04

    Score the forecasts and their ranges

    Every forecast is scored on what happened: percentage error, error against last year's number, bias, a Diebold-Mariano test (Bartlett weights, h-1 lags) and how often the 80% and 95% ranges held, in three windows of target months (before COVID, the COVID years, after).

    var = (e @ e) / n + 2 * sum((1 - k / h) * (e[k:] @ e[:n - k]) / n for k in range(1, h))
  5. Step 05

    Recount it without the libraries

    check.py reads the raw files and the stored forecasts with the csv module and recomputes the decomposition, the ADF and KPSS statistics, the trading-day regression, the four hand-coded benchmarks at all 188 origins and every score, test and coverage figure, solving each fit by Gauss-Jordan elimination: 47,425 numbers to a relative tolerance of 1e-8. The statsmodels forecasts are checked by properties only.

    b, inv = ols(X, y); e = [t - sum(c * x for c, x in zip(b, row)) for row, t in zip(X, y)]
04 / What it found

Results

Two series, two shapes. Census monthly sales, 415 months from January 1992 to July 2026, no gaps or duplicates. Furniture stores sold $9.8 billion a month in 2019 and $11.3 billion in 2025, with a long fall in 2008-2009 and a jump in 2021. Office, stationery and gift stores sold $2.3 billion and $2.4 billion, down from a peak of $5.2 billion in December 1999, and the category is changing shape: office supplies and stationery stores were 58% of the total in 2005 and 26% in 2025, the rest being gift, novelty and souvenir stores. April 2020 was 60% and 62% below April 2019.

Seasonality: bigger for office and gifts, steadier for furniture, and not constant. December is 19% above an average month for furniture and 47% for office and gifts, yet before COVID the seasonal share of the variation (strength, 0 to 1) is 0.92 for furniture against 0.86, because furniture's pattern is more regular. The December peak of office and gifts fell from 1.59 to 1.33 between 1992-2005 and 2006-2019. With 2020-2026 included the strengths fall to 0.64 and 0.71, the cost of the COVID months. Calendar effects are real but small: a month with one more Sunday sells less, a leap February sells more, and Saturdays do not differ from weekdays (the table: change in a month's sales, 12-month changes 1993-2019; t = -3.8 and -3.2 for a Sunday, 0.2 and -1.2 for a Saturday, 3.6 and 1.9 for a leap February; the three terms explain 8% and 5% of them).

Stationarity says difference twice: once, and once seasonally. On the 1992-2009 window (all the model choice may see) the log level of furniture sales cannot reject a unit root (ADF -2.05) and KPSS rejects stationarity (1.52); so does the seasonal difference alone (KPSS 0.85). Only the first plus seasonal difference passes both tests, for both series (ADF -4.80 and -5.72, KPSS 0.05 and 0.04), so d = 1 and D = 1. For office and gifts the tests disagree on the level (ADF -3.58 rejects a unit root, KPSS 1.24 rejects stationarity): seasonality alone looks like non-stationarity to KPSS. The AIC grid on that window picked SARIMA (1,1,2)(1,1,1)12 for both series.

Does ARIMA beat "same month last year"? For furniture, yes; for office and gifts, no. Every month from December 2009 to July 2025 (188 origins), each model is refitted on the months up to then and forecasts the next twelve. For target months January 2010 to February 2020, SARIMA's average error is 36% below last year's number for furniture (46% below one month ahead, 26% twelve months ahead, Diebold-Mariano p = 0.002 at 12). For office and gifts it is 3% above it: a gain at one month (12%, p = 0.083) and none at 6 or 12 months (p = 0.91 at 12). The table gives the mean absolute percentage error.

The two regression benchmarks lose, and not because of the seasonality: fitted on all months since 1992 a trend line is +13% and +28% off on average, because it does not survive the 2008-2009 fall in the level; on the last 60 months (a window chosen after seeing that) it is still worse than last year's number. The ranges hold before COVID, then miss by tens of standard errors. Before COVID SARIMA's 80% range held 91% and 87% of the time (a little too wide). Forecast in February 2020, it put furniture's April 2020 at $9.70 billion (95% range $9.15-10.28 billion); the month was $3.78 billion, 32 standard errors below (office and gifts: 22). Of the next twelve months, 3 (furniture) and 7 (office and gifts) fell outside the 95% range. After 2022 the range recovers, but the version that treats the lockdown as missing is overconfident.

The break echoes, and "last year's number" wins again afterwards. In the COVID years (target months March 2020 to December 2021) every model fails: the MAPE of the seven models other than the all-history regression runs from 14.8% to 25.1% for furniture and from 23.4% to 31.9% for office and gifts, against 2.6-6.2% and 3.1-4.0% before. ETS and SARIMA carry the crash forward as a lower level: from an origin of April 2020 furniture's SARIMA forecast for the next April is $2.56 billion. Last year's number repeats it a year later. Treating March-June 2020 as missing once the origin has passed them (possible only in hindsight) removes most of it (the gap model is SARIMA with trading days and those months missing). From 2022 on, though, last year's number has the lowest error of all (4.33% and 5.69%).

05 / What comes next

Next

  • Add intervention terms for 2008-2009 and 2020-2021 (and Easter and holiday effects) and see whether SARIMA then beats last year's number after 2022 too.
  • Run the same test on the other store types in the same survey, to see which categories gain from a model and which are already well forecast by last year's number.
  • Use the data as first released at each origin (real-time vintages), since the Census estimates are revised.
[ Note ]

Nominal dollars and today's vintage of the estimates (they are revised), not what was published at each forecast origin. The model orders came from AIC on 1992-2009 only; the lockdown-gap model uses hindsight (March-June 2020, picked by eye) and the 60-month regression window was chosen after seeing the all-history version fail. The office series is now mostly gift stores. No holiday or Easter effects. Source: US Census Bureau Monthly Retail Trade Survey via FRED, public domain.
