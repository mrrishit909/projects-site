What Makes a Hacker News Story Reach 100 Points?

Of 703,300 Hacker News stories in 27 sampled months, 5.0% reach 100+ points (a proxy for the front page) and the top 1% hold 30% of all points. Known at submission, little separates them: AUC 0.63 on later months; the comment count would give 0.99, but only afterwards.

01 / The problem

What needed answering

Every day hundreds of stories are submitted to Hacker News and a few take off. What separates the stories that reach 100 points from the rest, how fast do points arrive, and can that be guessed at the moment of submission, from earlier months only?

The task follows GeeksforGeeks' "News Scraping and Analysis". Scraping news sites is not allowed here, so the stand-in is Hacker News through its public search API: 703,300 stories from 27 whole months, kept as counts and metadata, never the text of a linked page. The API has no front-page rank, so "front page" is a points threshold.

02 / What was built

The build

One question, five angles on 703,300 stories:

  • The audit: 3,312 polite requests in 6-hour windows, counts checked against the API's own, duplicates, missing values, resubmitted links.
  • The tail: how many points the top 1% hold, and the share of stories reaching each score.
  • Who reaches 100: hour, weekday, post kind, title and domain, each with its interval.
  • A model of the past: logistic regression and boosted trees trained on March 2018 to September 2022, tested on 12 later months, with calibration and four deliberately leaky variants.
  • Check: check.py recounts 2,418 numbers in plain Python, with no data or statistics library, after verifying the fitted coefficients satisfy the score equations.
03 / How it was done

Step by step

  1. Step 01

    Download whole months, politely

    The HN Search API returns at most 1000 hits a query, so each of 27 months (March, July, September of 2018 to 2026) is cut into 6-hour windows, halved if a window ever reports more hits than it returns (none did): 3,312 requests, one at a time, a descriptive User-Agent, about 102 MB of metadata that is never committed. A live probe re-reads the last 30 hours every 10 minutes, 12 times, to watch points arrive.

    mid = (lo + hi) // 2; stats["splits"] += 1; return window(lo, mid, out) + window(mid, hi, out)
  2. Step 02

    Audit and clean the titles

    703,300 rows reported, 703,300 kept, 0 duplicate ids, 0 missing points, 21 missing comment counts, no day under 40% of its month's median. The API keeps the current title, and moderators add tags such as "(2019)" or "[pdf]" to stories that took off; they are stripped before a title is measured and kept out of the honest model.

    TAG = re.compile(r"\s*(\((?:19|20)[0-9]{2}\)|\[(?:pdf|video|audio|slides|podcast)\])\s*$", re.I)
  3. Step 03

    Give each domain only its past

    A domain's hit rate is a strong clue and an easy leak. Each training story sees only the earlier training stories of its domain, smoothed toward the base rate with 20 pseudo-stories; test stories see the whole training period; domains with fewer than 20 training stories are pooled as "other".

    n0, h0 = cnt.get(d, 0), hit.get(d, 0); p = (h0 + SMOOTH * p0) / (n0 + SMOOTH); enc_tr[i] = math.log(p / (1 - p)) - base
  4. Step 04

    Fit on the past, score on the future

    Logistic regression by Newton's method with a ridge penalty, on hour, weekday, post kind, link or self post, title features, 30 frequent words and the domain's past rate; trained on 376,469 stories to September 2022, scored on 326,831 from March 2023 on: AUC, average precision, lift in the top decile, Brier skill against the training base rate, ten calibration groups. Boosted trees are fitted as a comparison.

    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-Xb @ b)); g = Xb.T @ (y - p) - pen * b; H = (Xb * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]).T @ Xb + np.diag(pen); s = np.linalg.solve(H, g); b += s
  5. Step 05

    Recount it without the libraries

    check.py reads the files with the csv module, rebuilds every feature and domain encoding, verifies that each stored coefficient satisfies the score equation of the penalised likelihood (the fit is unique, so this is the fit), and recomputes every metric, calibration group and monthly AUC: 2,418 numbers at a relative tolerance of 1e-9. The boosted trees' metrics are recomputed from their stored predictions.

    assert abs(v) / len(tr) < 1e-8, f"{model} {k}: score equation not satisfied, |grad|/n = {abs(v) / len(tr)}"; n_ok += 1
04 / What it found

Results

A heavy tail. The median story has 2 points, the 90th percentile 33, the 99th 339, the maximum 6,015. The top 1% of stories hold 30% of all points, the top 10% 83%, and 2.5% of stories hold half. 5.0% (35,248) reach 100 or more, a share that drifted from 4.1% to 6.1% by month (5.2% in the training months, 4.8% in the test months).

When: weekends, not hours. The hit rate by UTC hour stays between 4.6% and 5.6%, and the hourly pattern of the training months barely predicts that of the test months (correlation 0.10). Weekends are easier: Saturday 5.6%, Sunday 6.2%, against 4.7% on Tuesday to Thursday, and the gap persists in the test months. That is an association: weekends have fewer rival stories, so it is not advice.

What: not the usual advice. Show HN (3.4%) and Ask HN (2.6%) are below average, plain self posts almost never reach 100 (0.9%), shorter titles do a little better, and a question mark does worse. Launch HN, Y Combinator's own category, reaches 100+ in 51% of 405 stories, an effect the API cannot show to be organic. A "(2019)"-type tag is on 7.1% of 100+ stories and 3.6% of the rest, because moderators add it to stories that took off: a leak, kept out of the model.

How fast: within a day. The API gives only a snapshot of points, so a probe re-read the last 30 hours every 10 minutes, 12 times, on 2026-10-04 (1:42 to 3:32 am US Eastern on a Sunday, a quiet slice). Stories under an hour old gained a point within the next hour or two in 31% of cases; those over 24 hours old in 4%. Stories already at 50+ points gained a median 7.4% of their points an hour at 3 to 6 hours old, 2.5% at 6 to 12 and 0.7% at 12 to 24. Points of stories more than a day old barely move, so months-old snapshots are close to final; none of 827 stories left the index in two hours. In the table, Gained is the share of stories that gained a point and Rate the median percent of their points that stories already at 50+ gained an hour.

Guessing at submission time works a little. Trained on the past and tested on 12 later months, the logistic model reaches AUC 0.628; the 10% of stories it ranks highest hold 18.3% of the 100+ stories (lift 1.83), and boosted trees are about the same (0.636). Each step adds a little (kind 0.540, time 0.548, title 0.593, domain 0.628). The starred rows are leaks: the comment count, known only afterwards, lifts AUC to 0.991; a domain rate computed on all months or a random split also flatter the model. Calibrated, but blind to drift. Predicted chances match what happened within 0.9 points in nine of ten groups, and the top group is over-predicted (10.9% against 8.8%). The base rate fell from 5.2% to 4.8%, and the model, fitted on the past, keeps predicting 5.2% on average; monthly AUC stays between 0.608 and 0.643. 49% of test stories come from a domain seen 20+ times in training; stories from other domains reach 100+ more often (5.9% against 4.1%).

05 / What comes next

Next

  • Add the points a story has after its first hour (a live system would have them), by polling the API for a few days, and see how much of the 0.99 that the comment count gave away can honestly be recovered.
  • Read the title's meaning with text embeddings instead of 30 frequent words, and check whether the gain survives the time split.
  • Poll the front-page tag every few minutes for a month to measure the real front page, its rank and how long stories stay.
[ Note ]

'Front page' is a points threshold (100+): the API has no rank. Points are a snapshot of 2026-10-04 (late September 2026 stories are four days old); deleted and flagged stories are missing; three months a year are sampled; titles are the current, moderator-edited ones. Associations, not advice.
