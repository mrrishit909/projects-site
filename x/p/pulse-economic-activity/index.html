Can Ships Tell You About a Slump Before the Statistics Do?

A motion-first map of synthetic world trade: 45,758 voyages rebuilt from noisy vessel-tracking and port feeds, port congestion from dwell times, and a regional activity index that flagged an East Asia slump 77 days before the official figure showed it (and missed Europe's smaller one).

01 / The problem

What needed answering

Official activity figures arrive weeks after the month they describe. Ships do not wait: a slump in demand shows up in fewer cargoes sailing toward a region, a port problem shows up in ships waiting at anchor. But shipping data is messy: vessels change identifiers, port names come in a dozen spellings, cargo is never reported directly. Can a pipeline turn two noisy feeds into a signal that reads ahead of the official figure, and say how sure it is?

The book's rule for this volume is that the website is the product: motion must show state, time and causality. Blueprint 4 of ten, built as its demo scenario end to end.

02 / What was built

The build

A Next.js site that runs entirely in the browser on a generated bundle, plus a Fastify API on PostgreSQL/PostGIS behind the same interface, started by one docker compose up.

  • A synthetic world: nine regional activity factors with a hidden demand shock, 52 lanes between 34 ports, ship-sized cargoes, berth queues and a berth outage, and two noisy feeds (AIS and port authority).
  • Entity resolution by IMO check digit, port alias and fuzzy name, and MMSI bindings learned from coincident rows.
  • A flow engine: calls, voyages, and cargo estimated from draught with a confidence on every figure.
  • Port congestion from dwell-time distributions, and a regional pulse from a dynamic factor model whose every value splits into its sources.
  • Nowcasts of the official figure (bridge regression and boosted trees), evaluated month by month on what was known then.
  • A map that morphs between globe and flat map, with every ship at sea drawn on its route for the day on screen.
03 / How it was done

Step by step

  1. Step 01

    A world with a truth behind it

    The generator draws nine regional activity factors that drift slowly and share a global component, and plants a demand slump in East Asia from 16 February 2026 (Europe follows two weeks later at a third of the size). Lane demand follows the destination's activity; departures come in ship-sized cargoes; every port has berth queues sized for about 65% use, and at Ningbo-Zhoushan 70% of discharge berths close from 23 February to 23 March. Then the feeds: vessel tracking with MMSIs, name variants and typos (3% of ships change MMSI when re-flagged), and port-authority rows with IMOs that are sometimes blank or carry a broken check digit, and seven ways to spell each port.

  2. Step 02

    Who is that ship, and where is it?

    Ports resolve by an alias dictionary, then Jaro-Winkler for dropped letters; positions resolve by a 25-nautical-mile geofence. Ships resolve by IMO when the check digit holds, otherwise by fuzzy name among ships that called at that port within 40 days, and AIS rows inherit the ship of a coincident port row. Scored against the truth: 99.8% of port names right, 97.8% of port-feed rows on the right ship (2.0% left unresolved rather than guessed, 0.12% wrong), 99.6% of AIS rows. From that, 98.6% of the true voyages come back, with 99.9% precision.

  3. Step 03

    Cargo nobody reports

    Tonnage is inferred from draught by a regression per ship class, fitted on the 25% of calls that carry a customs declaration. Median error 2.1%, 90th percentile 5.5%. The residual becomes the confidence shown next to every flow: crude on 17 March 2026 is 2.84 Mt a day plus or minus 5 kt.

  4. Step 04

    When ships wait

    For each port and day, the median dwell of ships that arrived in the last 7 days is compared with the 90 days before, as a robust z. At Ningbo in the week to 17 March ships waited a median 6.4 days against 1.6: z = 33.5. On the map the port pulses faster the higher its score, always below once a second; with reduced motion the pulse becomes a still ring whose width carries the level.

  5. Step 05

    A pulse with its sources

    Each region's index comes from five daily series (power demand, road freight, cargo departing toward it, cargo arriving, exports) through a one-factor dynamic factor model: principal component for the loadings, AR(1) for the factor, and a Kalman filter that skips missing days and carries, for every value, the exact contribution of each series. Against the hidden truth it tracks at a correlation of 0.79 to 0.95 in eight regions, and only 0.46 in Latin America, whose ports mostly export.

    P[t] = 1 / (1 / Pp + Σ λᵢ² / Rᵢ)                     // information form
c[i] = P[t] * (φ c[i] / Pp + λᵢ xᵢ / Rᵢ)            // contribution of series i; Σ c[i] = m[t] exactly
  6. Step 06

    Ahead of the official figure, honestly

    Fitted only on data from before the shock and filtered day by day, the East Asia index crossed its alarm level on 27 February 2026. The official indicator showed the drop when March was published on 15 May: 77 days later. Over 144 region-months, each nowcast made on the last day of its month with only what existed then, the root-mean-square error was 1.41 index points for boosted trees, 2.35 for the factor-model bridge and 3.94 for the last published value. The bridge's 80% interval held 74%, a little narrow, and in Latin America the bridge did worse than simply repeating the last figure. Europe's smaller slump was never flagged.

  7. Step 07

    Motion that carries state

    A ship's place on screen is a function of the day: its position is the fraction of its voyage elapsed, computed on the GPU, so rewinding sails it backwards. The globe and the flat map are one mesh with a morph uniform, so routes and ports stay put while it unrolls. Every chart's time cursor is driven from the same clock as the map in each frame. A list view carries every figure without WebGL.

  8. Step 08

    The same product behind an API

    The Docker stack serves the same site against a Fastify API on PostGIS: anonymous calls get a free tier 30 days behind, tokens carry roles, row-level security keeps each tenant's alerts and private uploads apart, mutations take idempotency keys, the audit log is hash-chained, and spans go to an OpenTelemetry collector. A congestion alert on Ningbo, replayed over two years, fires once (27 February 2026, z 6.7) and lands as e-mail in a local mail sink. Two failures are built in and recover: the AIS feed stopping and the data service going down.

Where it falls short. The 80% nowcast interval held 74%. Europe's smaller slump went unflagged. The 14-day flow intervals held only 51% for iron ore and 62% for grain. The pulse barely tracks Latin America (correlation 0.46).

Checks and performance. Unit coverage 98.9% of statements, including tests that changing data after a nowcast's date leaves it unchanged and that the contributions add up exactly. Every API response is validated against the OpenAPI document. A real GPU holds 60 frames a second both with 48,292 ships drawn and with ten times as many. The API served 2,139 reads a second at p95 21 ms and ingested 66,333 resolved rows a second.

05 / What comes next

Next

  • Pool the factor model across regions so exporters such as Latin America borrow strength from their buyers' signals.
  • Calibrate the nowcast and flow intervals from their own backtests, since both run narrow (74% and as low as 51%).
  • An alarm rule tied to each region's own volatility, so a third-sized shock like Europe's can be caught without more false alarms.
  • Sea routes for the ribbons and voyage times, and a streaming ingestion path once a real feed needs it.
[ Note ]

Built from blueprint 04 of a written build book (Volume V, motion-first products) as one vertical slice. Everything is synthetic: ports sit at real coordinates, but every vessel, voyage, cargo, activity series, official figure, shock and berth outage comes from a generator in the repository with a hidden true process. The models never see the truth; it is drawn only to judge them. No language model is used. Not built: real AIS, port or customs data, Kafka and stream processing, ClickHouse, Terraform and cloud deployment, single sign-on, billing, sea routing (ribbons follow great circles).
